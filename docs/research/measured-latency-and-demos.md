# Measured latency, reliability and UX evidence for small BLE "shared state" systems (3–10 ESP32-S3 nodes, one room)

Scope note: figures below come from (a) pages whose full text was fetched, or (b) search-result snippets when the full text was blocked (marked **[snippet]**). Forum posts and single-user reports are marked **[anecdote]**. Numbers I derived myself are marked **[estimate]**. Target platform constraints: ESP32-S3, BLE 5.0, ESP-IDF v5.5.4, Wi-Fi station stays connected, indoors, 3–10 nodes, 1–2 hops.

---

## Key Question 1 — Bluetooth SIG mesh: measured one-hop / multi-hop latency and delivery ratio in small networks

### Takeaway
Independent testbeds on nRF52840 hardware put a single-hop, unsegmented Bluetooth Mesh message at a median of ~4.5 ms (mean ~8 ms, tail to ~110 ms) when the relay path is quiet, and at ~25 ms mean / ~50 ms 90th percentile when 3 nodes publish concurrently with the default 20 ms advertising interval and 3 network transmissions; acknowledged round trips are 30–70 ms. Delivery ratio for unsegmented, unacknowledged messages is 98–100 % with network transmit count ≥ 2; segmentation (payload > 11 B) roughly triples latency.

### Cited Findings

**Testbed A — ETH Zürich / WiMob 2023 (Cortesi, Vogt, Reinschmidt, Magno), 10 × nRF52840 (Würth Proteus III), Zephyr v3.1.99, chain topology, GPIO-toggle timestamping on a logic analyser at 1 MSps, 1000 cycles, 1-byte broadcast, TX power −40 dBm, relays always scanning**
- 1-hop latency: mean 7.86 ms (Table I; body text says 8.86 ms, a discrepancy in the paper), median 4.54 ms, std 11.43 ms, min 3.17 ms, max 111.42 ms — [ar5iv 2310.14681, Sec. IV-A1, Table I](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.14681); abstract confirms "median latency is 4.54 ms" — [arXiv abs 2310.14681](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.14681)
- 9-hop latency: mean 70.57 ms, median 63.44 ms, std 35.13 ms, min 37.94 ms, max 282.16 ms (Table I) — [ar5iv 2310.14681](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.14681)
- Single-hop latencies cluster in three levels between ~4.2 and ~5.4 ms (Fig. 4a) — i.e., the three advertising channels / advertising-event timing dominate — [ar5iv 2310.14681](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.14681)
- Packet delivery ratio is *not reported*; network transmit count/interval and scan parameters are not reported — [ar5iv 2310.14681](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.14681)
- Bluetooth Mesh node power draw 6.2 mA at 3.3 V (relay always scanning) — [arXiv abs 2310.14681](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.14681)

**Testbed B — arXiv 2106.04230 (2021; search summary attributes it to Toshiba Research), 20 × nRF52840 DK, Nordic nRF5 SDK for Mesh, two floors (~600 m²), up to 4 hops, 100 iterations, one message per 1000 ms. Default settings (Table I): 0 dBm, advInterval 20 ms, scanInterval 2000 ms, all nodes relay, 3 advertising events at the source and 2 at relays, 11-byte unsegmented messages**
- Round-trip, 8-node single-hop group (1 controller + 7 slaves): **unicast (acknowledged) 100 % reliability, mean 133.77 ms, max 192.57 ms; group mode 99.07 % reliability, mean 38.57 ms, max 43.45 ms** (Table II) — [ar5iv 2106.04230](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.04230)
- Round-trip, 15-node multi-hop group: unicast 100 %, mean 152.27 ms, max 253.62 ms; group 89.6 %, mean 51.62 ms, max 94.37 ms (Table II) — [ar5iv 2106.04230](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.04230)
- Many-to-many, one-way, default parameters: **3 senders mean 24.94 ms / 90th pct 50.41 ms; 7 senders mean 37.89 ms / 90th pct 79.59 ms** (Sec. III-C) — [ar5iv 2106.04230](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.04230)
- Segmentation, 3 senders: unsegmented 11 B mean 24.94 ms / p90 50.41 ms; segmented 19 B mean 76.32 ms / p90 101.67 ms (Sec. III-D) — [ar5iv 2106.04230](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.04230)
- Scan/adv tuning (Table III, mean / p90): scanInterval 2000 ms + advInterval 20 ms (default) → 3 senders 24.94/50.41 ms; **1000 ms + 10 ms → 17.09/31.18 ms** (−31.5 %); 500 ms + 10 ms → 23.22/50.69 ms. 7 senders: 37.89/79.59 → 23.22/50.7 → 26.69/56.5 ms — [ar5iv 2106.04230](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.04230)
- Reducing the relay set (7 senders): all relay 37.89 ms mean → half relay 28.37 ms (−25 %) → quarter relay 29.35 ms (Sec. III-H) — [ar5iv 2106.04230](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.04230)
- 50-byte messages: legacy adv one-to-many round trip mean 204.88 ms / p90 343.5 ms vs Nordic proprietary Instaburst extended adv 76.35 / 184 ms; many-to-many 3 senders one-way 120.8 → 27.28 ms (Sec. III-F) — [ar5iv 2106.04230](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.04230)

**Testbed C — Nordic Semiconductor large-scale office test, 99 × nRF52840, nRF Connect SDK v2.5.1 with SoftDevice Controller, ceiling-mounted, 2–3 m spacing, TTL 7 (unack) / 5 (ack), Network Transmit Count (NTC) 0–2 at 20 ms interval, Relay Retransmit Count (RRC) 0–2 at 20 ms, 100 iterations per node**
- Unacknowledged unsegmented unicast reliability (Table 5): NTC 0/RRC 0 all relays → 100 % for all nodes; NTC 2/RRC 0 → 100 %; NTC 2/RRC 2 → 100 %; limited relays with NTC 0/RRC 0 → 100 % for most, 99 % for 17 nodes, 98 % for 2 nodes — [Nordic blog](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/nordic/nordic-blog/b/blog/posts/large-scale-bluetooth-mesh-testing)
- Acknowledged messages: even NTC 2/RRC 2 with all relays were "still insufficient to guarantee 100 % reliability"; best reliability at NTC 2/RRC 0 — [Nordic blog](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/nordic/nordic-blog/b/blog/posts/large-scale-bluetooth-mesh-testing)
- Per-node latency values are only in images (Figs 4–7); Nordic's Figure 6 mean RTT for acknowledged messages is "around 30 ms" for the closest nodes (per Nordic engineer in the follow-up thread) — [DevZone 121281](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/f/nordic-q-a/121281/ble-mesh-latency-measurements-not-matching-public-nordic-testing-data/535373)
- Fewer relays gave "significantly lower one-way latency" for nodes near the tester; acknowledged RTT with all relays and RRC = 2 was "significantly high" for distant nodes — [Nordic blog](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/nordic/nordic-blog/b/blog/posts/large-scale-bluetooth-mesh-testing)
- **[anecdote]** A user with 2 × nRF52840 DK, Zephyr controller, custom model, measured 60–70 ms round trip (publish → app-level ack) vs Nordic's ~30 ms; Nordic attributed the gap to the Zephyr controller vs SoftDevice Controller and asked for a re-test; retry counts changed reliability but not RTT — [DevZone 121281](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/f/nordic-q-a/121281/ble-mesh-latency-measurements-not-matching-public-nordic-testing-data/535373)

**Vendor note D — Silicon Labs "Bluetooth Mesh 1.1 Network Performance" (replaces AN1137), EFR32xG21/xG24, 256-node office with 4 Wi-Fi networks and "hundreds" of other BLE devices in range; multi-hop via 8 RF-isolated boxes chained to 6 hops; unicast tests used Net TX 3, Relay TX 3, 1 message / 1500 ms, 1000 sends, PDU 8–128 B; multicast Net TX 3, Relay TX 3, 1 msg / 3000 ms, 10/50/100/256 nodes**
- "Latency can be maintained below 200 milliseconds, even out to 6 hops" when payload < 16 bytes; reliability "greater than 99 %" — [SiLabs summary](https://docs.silabs.com/btmesh/10.0.0/btmesh-11-network-performance/05-summary)
- Latency rises with network size and payload size, payload having the larger effect; per-hop numbers are only in chart images — [SiLabs results](https://docs.silabs.com/btmesh/11.0.1/btmesh-11-network-performance/04-testing-and-results); setup — [SiLabs test network](https://docs.silabs.com/btmesh/11.0.1/btmesh-11-network-performance/03-test-network-and-conditions)
- **[snippet]** In Silicon Labs' cross-protocol comparison (192 nodes, every node a relay) Bluetooth mesh latency "peaks at 60 ms, spreading past 250 ms" while Zigbee clustered near 80 ms; Silicon Labs advises short (≤ 11 B) messages especially for multicast — [SiLabs mesh performance page](https://www.silabs.com/wireless/multiprotocol/mesh-performance)

**Other papers (not fully read)**

**Stack defaults relevant to the ESP32 build**
- Zephyr/NCS `CONFIG_BT_MESH_NETWORK_TRANSMIT_COUNT` default 2 (range 0–7) = retransmissions in addition to the first transmission — [Zephyr Kconfig](https://docs.zephyrproject.org/2.7.5/reference/kconfig/CONFIG_BT_MESH_NETWORK_TRANSMIT_COUNT.html); "0 → 1 transmission, 1 → 2 transmissions" — [DevZone 87449](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/f/nordic-q-a/87449/ble-mesh-transmit-count-on-zephyr)
- Mesh Network Transmit / Relay Retransmit: count is 3 bits (1 transmission … 1 + 7 retransmissions); interval is a 5-bit value × 10 ms step, 10–320 ms; Silicon Labs: "Only in areas with very few relays should the network/relay repetitions be enabled"; TTL 0 = never relayed; a client controlling a room in direct range should publish with TTL = 0 — [SiLabs networking principles](https://docs.silabs.com/btmesh/9.0.2/btmesh-network-optimization/03-bluetooth-mesh-networking-principles)
- ESP-BLE-MESH FAQ: enable Relay on all nodes when the network is sparse; relaying requires TTL > 1; segmented messages retried up to 4 times; incomplete-reassembly timer ~10 s; default client response timeout 4 s (`CONFIG_BLE_MESH_CLIENT_MSG_TIMEOUT`); "Currently only Wi-Fi station mode supports the coexistence"; enabling PSRAM/coexistence options in menuconfig is recommended when Wi-Fi throughput drops — [ESP-IDF v5.5.1 ESP-BLE-MESH FAQ](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/v5.5.1/esp32s3/api-guides/esp-ble-mesh/ble-mesh-faq.html)
- ESP FAQ: lower layer does not segment messages ≤ 11 bytes; application-layer max 384 B; fast provisioning ~100 nodes in 50–60 s — [Espressif FAQ](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-faq/en/latest/application-solution/ble-mesh-development-framework.html)
- **[anecdote]** An ESP32-C6 GATT-proxy user reports that the delay sits between advertising enqueue and actual transmission in the mesh forwarding path (thread unanswered at fetch time; esp32.com blocked full read) — [esp32.com p=154569](https://esp32.com/viewtopic.php?p=154569)

### Inferences
- For 3–10 nodes in one room (all within direct range), the relevant numbers are the *single-hop / direct-range* rows: expect ~5 ms best case, ~25 ms mean and ~50 ms p90 when several nodes publish at once with the standard 20 ms advertising interval and 3 network transmissions. With TTL = 0 (no relaying, as Silicon Labs recommends for in-room control) and NTC = 2 at 20 ms, the broadcast burst lasts ~40–60 ms plus 0–10 ms adv random delay per event **[estimate]**.
- The 7-sender case (mean 37.9 ms, p90 79.6 ms) is the closest published proxy for "5–10 holders broadcasting concurrently" and already brushes the ~100 ms perceptual limit at the tail (see Q4). Shortening advInterval to 10 ms reduced mean latency by ~31–38 % in Testbed B — ESP-IDF exposes the mesh advertising interval, so this is a direct knob.
- Keep the payload ≤ 11 B (one RGB triple + flags + sender id fits easily): segmentation roughly triples latency (25 → 76 ms mean) and adds the 4-retry SAR machinery.
- Acknowledged (unicast) mesh messaging costs ~130 ms RTT in an 8-node group and should be avoided for live state; group/publish mode (unacked) at 99 % reliability with 38 ms RTT is the right mode.
- The max values (111 ms at 1 hop in Testbed A, 192 ms unicast RTT in Testbed B) mean a mesh-based design must tolerate occasional 100 ms+ outliers; periodic re-publish hides them (see Q6).

### Gaps
- No independent measurement of Espressif's ESP-BLE-MESH stack latency on ESP32/ESP32-S3 was found (Espressif's FAQ gives none; the C6 proxy thread is unanswered). All measured numbers are nRF52840 (Zephyr, nRF5 SDK, SDC) or EFR32.
- Silicon Labs' per-hop and per-payload latency values exist only as chart images; the text gives just "< 200 ms to 6 hops" and "> 99 %".
- Baert et al. 2018 per-hop RTT tables could not be read (MDPI/PMC/HAL blocked).
- No measurement of Bluetooth Mesh with a *simultaneously connected Wi-Fi station on the same SoC* was found.

## Key Question 2 — BLE advertising/beacon reception: per-advertisement reception probability, latency vs. advertising interval, Wi-Fi coexistence on ESP32

### Takeaway
With a scanner listening continuously and a single advertiser in a shielded box, per-advertisement delivery is ~96–99 % (loss comes from the scanner's channel-hop gaps); with 2–18 advertisers at 100 ms intervals collisions add a few percent loss, partially offset by the capture effect. Worst-case discovery latency is one advertising interval (plus 0–10 ms advDelay) when the scan window equals the scan interval; ESP32 users report advertising loss rising from ~3 % to 20 %+ with long 100 % loss streaks when Wi-Fi coexistence is active, but no controlled measurement exists.

### Cited Findings

**Reception probability**
- nRF54L15 scanner, continuous scanning, shielded box ("no other BLE packets were detected"): PDR at scan interval 30 ms = 96.1 % (1M PHY) / 94.6 % (Coded); 50 ms = 97.6 / 96.0 %; 100 ms = 98.9 / 97.1 %; 250 ms = 98.9 / 98.0 %. Nordic says it has no GAP-layer PDR benchmark; a Nordic engineer called 1 % PER "good" and cited the RF-PHY test limit of PER < 30.8 % — [DevZone 126337](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/f/nordic-q-a/126337/benchmark-for-ble-receiver-performance/558157)
- **[snippet]** Faraday-box study of 2–14 advertisers: the capture effect raised advertisement delivery by 2.95 % on average at 14 devices (peak 7.8 %); simulations up to 1000 advertisers suggest up to +25 % PDR from capture; collision probability rises with device count — [HAL hal-03165168 "Be aware of the capture effect"](https://hal-imt-atlantique.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-03165168) (full text blocked)
- **[snippet]** *Sensors* 2017, 17(9):1988: controlled measurements with 2–18 advertisers at 100 ms and 10 ms advertising intervals; scanner configuration affects non-detection; back-off matters above ~100 advertisers — [MDPI 17/9/1988](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/17/9/1988) (full text blocked)
- PRP paper (De, Vasisht, Sundaram, Kravets, 2023): BLE advertisement reception vs RSSI is probabilistic with substantial variance; RSSI loses correlation with distance beyond ~2 m — [arXiv 2306.01688](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01688); **[snippet]** the same paper states the ≤ 31 B advertising payload keeps collisions low "with up to 200 or more co-located devices", that passive scanners add no interference, and that a 2022 ACM MSWiM study saw 13–40 % fewer packets delivered per second under interference — [search snippet, same paper PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.01688)
- **[snippet]** A simulation of scanning batches up to 1600 advertisers found correlated successive collisions dominate failure probability and that failure probability "is not highly sensitive to the advertising interval" — [core.ac.uk 335348076](https://core.ac.uk/download/335348076.pdf)

**Latency vs. advertising interval / scan window**
- Kindt et al. (arXiv 1509.04366), 2 × nRF51822 dongles, BLESSED stack, single channel, scan interval 2.42 s / window 0.59 s, advertising interval swept 0.1–3.0 s in 10 ms steps, 100 repetitions each (29 100 runs): "our predicted bound is always reached, but never exceeded"; max excess over the bound 8.7 ms (attributed to USB latency). For Ta ≤ ds the worst-case discovery latency is ⌈(Ts − ds)/Ta⌉ · Ta, i.e., bounded within one scan interval — [ar5iv 1509.04366](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1509.04366)
- **[snippet]** Shan et al. 2022: discovery latency can become unbounded when the advertising interval equals an integer multiple of the scan interval minus the mean random delay — [ACM 10.1145/3560089.3560097](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F3560089.3560097)
- **[snippet]** Periodic-deterministic advertising has low discovery latency but is "susceptible to persistent collisions"; random-delay advertising avoids persistent collisions at the cost of latency — [arXiv 2312.15650](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.15650v1)
- advDelay: each advertising event is advInterval plus a pseudo-random 0–10 ms delay generated by the Link Layer (Core Spec Vol 6 Part B §4.4.2.2); Nordic says the distribution "can be implementation dependent"; one user observed values clustered at 5–7 ms; a TI forum claims 625 µs steps (unverified) — [DevZone 85629](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/f/nordic-q-a/85629/nature-of-pseudo-random-delay-advdelay-0-10-ms-added-to-the-advertising-interval); [Novel Bits](https://novelbits.io/bluetooth-le-advertising-interval-vs-period/)

**ESP32 Wi-Fi + BLE coexistence (ESP32-S3)**
- ESP-IDF v5.5 coexistence guide (ESP32-S3): Wi-Fi STA "Connected" is listed as stable with BLE Scan, Advertising and Connected; in connected state "the coexistence period starts at the Target Beacon Transmission Time (TBTT) and is more than 100 ms"; Wi-Fi CONNECTED + BLE CONNECTED share 50 % / 50 % time slices; "in every N BLE Advertising events, there is always one event with high priority" that can pre-empt Wi-Fi; recommended to keep Wi-Fi connectionless power-save parameters at defaults and to run the Wi-Fi task and BLE controller/host tasks on different CPUs; BLE Mesh must report `ESP_COEX_BLE_ST_MESH_CONFIG / TRAFFIC / STANDBY` status to the coexistence module — [ESP-IDF v5.5 ESP32-S3 coexist](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/v5.5/esp32s3/api-guides/coexist.html)
- **[snippet]** ESP-IDF coexistence docs (ESP32 classic page): "BLE SCAN may be interrupted by Wi-Fi and Wi-Fi releases RF resources before the end of the current BLE scan window"; `CONFIG_BTDM_CTRL_FULL_SCAN_SUPPORTED` lets BLE reacquire the radio within the current scan window — [ESP-IDF v5.3.1 ESP32 coexist](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/v5.3.1/esp32/api-guides/coexist.html)
- **[anecdote, snippet]** ESP32-S3 user: ~3 % BLE packet loss with standalone BLE, "20 %+, with some long streaks of 100 % loss" once Wi-Fi (esp-matter) is active; Espressif staff reply: BLE scanning is not a "surefire protocol where you can expect a scanner to receive 100 % of advertising packets", suggested repeating advertisements with ~35 ms spacing, and recommended a connection with acknowledgements if zero drops are required — [esp32.com p=126397](https://esp32.com/viewtopic.php?p=126397)
- **[anecdote, snippet]** ESP32-C3 SW-coexistence example user saw poor scan results and asked how to size scan interval/window under coexistence; no clear answer — [esp32.com p=100229](https://esp32.com/viewtopic.php?p=100229)
- Espressif advisory AR2024-001: with Wi-Fi in HT40 under coexistence, some Android phones cannot receive SCAN_RSP PDUs from ESP32 (affects ESP-IDF v5.0.4–5.0.5, v5.1.1–5.1.2, v5.2 betas); keep Wi-Fi at HT20 or use fixed versions — [Espressif advisory PDF](https://www.espressif.com/sites/default/files/advisory_downloads/AR2024-001%20Advisory%20Concerning%20Some%20Android%20Phones%20Being%20Unable%20to%20Scan%20the%20Advertising%20PDUs%20Sent%20by%20ESP32%20Bluetooth%20LE%20EN.pdf)

### Inferences
- With scan window = scan interval (continuous scan) and a 20 ms advertising interval, the time from "state change → first advertisement on air" is ≤ 20 ms + 0–10 ms advDelay, and each advertisement has roughly a 96–99 % chance of being heard by an idle scanner in a clean room (Nordic shielded-box numbers; real rooms will be lower) **[estimate]**. Three repeats spaced 20 ms therefore give ≈ 1 − 0.04³ ≈ 99.99 % delivery *in a clean room*, but the ESP32 coexistence anecdote (100 % loss streaks) means repeats must be spread over > 100 ms, i.e., longer than one coexistence period (TBTT-based, > 100 ms), to survive a Wi-Fi slice.
- Espressif's own staff advice for lossy scanning under coexistence (repeat with ~35 ms spacing, or use a connection) matches the design in Q6: periodic state re-broadcast at a fixed cadence rather than one-shot events.
- Keep Wi-Fi at HT20 on the S3 and pin Wi-Fi and BLE tasks to different cores, as the coexistence guide recommends.

### Gaps
- No controlled measurement of ESP32-family advertisement reception probability or scan loss with a connected Wi-Fi station was found; only forum anecdotes and qualitative docs.
- No paper gives per-advertisement reception probability for N = 2–10 advertisers at 20/50/100 ms intervals as a table (the HAL and Sensors 2017 papers do, but their full text was blocked).
- Measured "state change → reception" latency at 20/50/100 ms advertising intervals with continuous scanning was not found as a direct experiment; Kindt's bound (one interval when Ta ≤ ds) is the best-supported statement.

## Key Question 3 — BLE connections: notification latency at 7.5–30 ms connection intervals

### Takeaway
No rigorous published end-to-end measurement was found; vendor engineers state that at a 7.5 ms connection interval a notification arrives within ~1–7.5 ms (uniformly distributed over the interval) on a clean link, that retransmissions add multiples of the interval, and that stack buffering/host-side handling can push observed values to tens of milliseconds.

### Cited Findings
- Nordic engineer: at 7.5 ms interval, "values of roughly 1 to 7.5 ms would be expected" because a packet queued just after a connection event waits almost a full interval; peripheral slave latency (skipping events) adds delay; the poster measured 56 ms+ (unexplained) — [DevZone 68990](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/f/nordic-q-a/68990/sending-packet-latency-over-ble)
- Nordic: a 7.5 ms interval gives a typical worst case of about 7.5 ms on a good link plus processing overhead; stacks buffer several packets for the next event — [DevZone 81364](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/f/nordic-q-a/81364/ble-connection-interval/337459)
- Nordic recommended measurement method: toggle a GPIO on the peripheral when the notification is written and on the central when it is received, observe on an oscilloscope; packet loss can make latency N × 7.5 ms near max range; verify the negotiated interval, which can be silently overridden (one case pointed at ~85 ms) — [DevZone 58552](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/f/nordic-q-a/58552/measure-latency-on-nrf52-dk-nrf52832)
- **[anecdote]** A notification larger than 19 bytes took two connection intervals — [DevZone 69173](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/f/nordic-q-a/69173/transmitting-multiple-notifications-in-one-connection-interval)
- **[anecdote]** BLE HID mouse measured ~80 ms average latency despite a short interval, traced to Windows HID-over-BLE handling — [DevZone 119571](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/f/nordic-q-a/119571/unusually-high-ble-connection-latency/526353)
- Tosi et al. 2017 systematic review: connInterval is a multiple of 1.25 ms from 7.5 ms to 4.0 s; Gomez et al. (simulation) report one-way and round-trip ATT latency "from 0.6 ms to 10 ms" at reasonable BER (10⁻⁶–10⁻⁴); the review notes rigorous experimental evaluation is still lacking — [PMC5751532](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5751532/)
- ESP32-S3 coexistence: Wi-Fi CONNECTED + BLE CONNECTED → 50 % / 50 % time slices — [ESP-IDF v5.5 coexist](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/v5.5/esp32s3/api-guides/coexist.html)

### Inferences
- A star of GATT connections (one central, up to 9 peripherals) at 7.5–15 ms intervals would in principle deliver a state change in ~10–20 ms with acknowledgements, far below the mesh numbers — but on an ESP32-S3 with Wi-Fi connected, each connection competes for the 50 % BLE slice, and every hop through the central doubles the interval cost **[estimate]**. A connection design also needs discovery/pairing/reconnection logic that the broadcast designs avoid (see Q5 lessons).
- Espressif's staff recommend connections when zero drops are required under coexistence (Q2), so a hybrid (advertising for live state, connection for rare reliable config) is defensible.

### Gaps
- No paper or app note with measured notification latency distributions at 7.5/15/30 ms intervals on ESP32 or nRF hardware was retrievable (a 2023 IEEE BSN paper by Rajotte & Wooding exists at WPI but the server's certificate was rejected).
- No measurement of ESP32-S3 multi-connection (central with 5–9 peripherals) latency while Wi-Fi is connected.

## Key Question 4 — Human perception: what counts as "instant" for touch-to-light feedback, and jitter tolerance

### Takeaway
For a *discrete* touch-triggers-light event the evidence converges on: feedback within ~30 ms is judged simultaneous, ≤ 85–100 ms is "instant" for practical purposes (Nielsen's 0.1 s; Kaaresoja's 30–85 ms visual guideline), and quality ratings fall off between 100 and 150 ms. Continuous dragging is far more sensitive (JNDs of ~6–11 ms), but that is not the demo's interaction. Moderate jitter matters much less than mean latency and dropouts.

### Cited Findings
- Nielsen (after Miller 1968, Card et al. 1991): 0.1 s is the limit for the user to feel the system is "reacting instantaneously", "no special feedback is necessary except to display the result"; 1.0 s keeps the train of thought; 10 s is the attention limit — [NN/g response times](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/)
- Kaaresoja, Brewster & Lantz 2014, ACM TAP 11(2): delays 0–300 ms; point of subjective simultaneity 5 ms tactile, 19 ms audio, **32 ms visual**; recommended visual feedback latency **30–85 ms** (tactile 5–50, audio 20–70); perceived quality dropped significantly between 100 and 150 ms for visual (70–100 ms for tactile/audio); all modalities rated significantly worse at 300 ms — [Glasgow eprints 104653](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/104653/)
- **[snippet]** Jota, Ng, Dietz & Wigdor, CHI 2013 "How fast is fast enough?": users are unable to perceive latency in response to tapping below ~24 ms (as summarised in Deber et al. 2015's related work) — [CHI 2013 DOI 10.1145/2470654.2481317](https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2481317); secondary summary — [search summary](https://dcmpx.remotevs.com/com/tactuallabs/www/SL/papers/howMuchFasterIsFastEnoughCHI15.pdf)
- **[snippet]** Ng, Lepinski, Wigdor, Sanders & Dietz, UIST 2012 "Designing for low-latency direct-touch input": users could detect dragging latency as low as ~6 ms (another citing source says 5–10 ms); commercial touch devices measured at 60–200 ms end-to-end — [UIST 2012 DOI 10.1145/2380116.2380128](https://doi.org/10.1145/2380116.2380128); citing paper — [ualberta GI 2014](https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/%7Ewfb/publications/C-2014-GI-Latency.pdf)
- **[snippet]** Deber, Jota, Forlines & Wigdor, CHI 2015 "How much faster is fast enough?": JNDs (secondary citation) direct touch ≈ 11 ms dragging / 69 ms tapping; indirect ≈ 55 ms dragging / 96 ms tapping; an improvement as small as 8.3 ms is noticeable across a wide range of baselines; direct touch is more latency-sensitive because the finger-to-output gap is visible — [CHI 2015 DOI 10.1145/2702123.2702300](https://doi.org/10.1145/2702123.2702300); figures via [arXiv 2408.02525](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.02525)
- **[snippet]** Forch et al. 2017 "Are 100 ms fast enough?": latency perception thresholds for simple mouse interaction ≈ 60 ms; earlier guidelines proposed 100 ms for highly interactive systems — [Lübeck IMIS](https://imis.uni-luebeck.de/de/node/7124); Attig, Rauh, Franke & Krems 2017 "System latency guidelines then and now" (EPCE 2017, LNCS 10276, pp. 3–14) reviews the same 100 ms guideline — [Springer 10.1007/978-3-319-58475-1_4](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2F978-3-319-58475-1_4)
- **[snippet]** Kaaresoja & Anttila 2011 (tactile keypads): keypads with a constant 18 ms delay and the smallest delay variation were faster and produced fewer errors than conditions with wider delay variability — [IFIP INTERACT 2011](https://dl.ifip.org/IFIP-LNCS-6947/hal-01590872)
- **[snippet]** Pavlovych & Stuerzlinger 2010: "latency and dropouts have a strong effect on human performance; moderate amounts of jitter in latency do not change performance in a significant way in most cases" — [SFU VVISE](https://vvise.iat.sfu.ca/pubs/pavlovych2010effects)
- **[snippet]** Bath target-acquisition study (ACM TAP): large jitter amplitudes caused a small completion-time impairment that decreased as mean latency increased — [Bath research portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/how-changes-in-the-mean-latency-jitter-amplitude-and-jitter-frequ/)
- **[snippet]** Jack et al. 2016 (musical instruments): 0 ms rated better than 10 ms ± 3 ms jitter and 20 ms on six quality measures — [instrumentslab PDF](https://instrumentslab.org/data/andrew/jack_am2016.pdf)

### Inferences
- Design target for "touch-and-hold on A lights B": local feedback on A itself within one display frame (≤ 32 ms, the visual PSS), remote lighting of B within ≤ 85 ms median and ≤ 150 ms worst case (where quality ratings start to fall). Comparing to Q1/Q2: a direct-range advertising or mesh group message with 20 ms interval (mean 25 ms, p90 50 ms with 3 concurrent senders) sits inside the 30–85 ms band; the 7-sender p90 of ~80 ms is at its edge; acknowledged unicast mesh (~130 ms RTT) is outside it.
- Because the demo is a discrete on/off-with-mixing event rather than dragging, the 6–11 ms dragging JNDs do not apply; the person is not comparing their finger position with a cursor on the other device.
- Jitter of a few tens of ms is unlikely to be noticed as long as mean latency stays low and drops are hidden; what people *do* notice is a dropout (a holder whose colour never appears or flickers), which argues for redundancy over raw speed.
- Release latency matters too: when a finger lifts, the other screens should fade within the same ~100 ms budget; a receiver-side expiry of 2–3 missed re-broadcasts at 50 ms cadence (100–150 ms) is at the edge, so an explicit "released" flag sent several times is better than relying on expiry alone (see Q6) **[estimate]**.

### Gaps
- Primary texts of Ng 2012, Jota 2013 and Deber 2015 could not be fetched (ACM/Tactual Labs servers refused); JND numbers are second-hand.
- No study specifically on *remote* light feedback (touch on one object lights another object) was found; thresholds are transferred from on-screen virtual buttons.
- No quantitative jitter threshold for visual feedback; evidence is "moderate jitter is not significant" without an ms boundary.

## Key Question 5 — Comparable products and projects: transport chosen, node counts, reported latency, authors' lessons

### Takeaway
Hobby and festival "swarm light" projects overwhelmingly choose connectionless broadcast (ESP-NOW on ESP32; raw BLE advertising libraries exist but no finished multi-device colour-sync project on BLE advertising was found); commercial crowd wristbands (PixMob) use one-way, repeated IR/RF broadcast with hard-coded group IDs; consumer smart bulbs that care about latency moved to Thread mesh (Nanoleaf), with Bluetooth control reported as the laggy path. Reported lessons: converge on shared time or state rather than synchronising events, quantise to hide jitter, re-broadcast periodically, and expect RF assembly details to matter.

### Cited Findings

**Festival / wearable swarms (ESP-NOW)**
- "Self-synchronising LED badges using ESP-NOW" (ESP32-C3 + WS2812 rings, ~10 badges planned): chosen because badges need no pairing, controller or Wi-Fi network; each badge broadcasts its `millis()` every ~5 ms, quantised to 10 ms; a receiver adopts any higher time (time never goes backwards); rendering is quantised to 50 ms because at 10 ms steps "network jitter started to dominate"; effects are computed statelessly from shared time so nodes that agree on time render identically; no ACK/retransmit — periodic re-broadcast provides resilience; packet loss "irrelevant" except that a badge drifts until the next broadcast; sockets for the C3 modules caused unreliable reception, fixed by soldering directly ("At RF frequencies, 'it shouldn't matter' often means 'it definitely matters'"); "Perfect synchronisation is expensive", quantised sync is "cheap, robust, and good enough to look perfect"; no security — [Hackaday.io 205611](https://hackaday.io/project/205611-self-synchronising-led-badges-using-esp-now)
- Elektor Labs "Fireflies": ESP32 wearables using "ESPNow beacons to synchronise colors whenever they get near each other"; groups of matching colours form and a new member re-syncs the group colour; ESP8266 possible only "if care is taken with timing constraints" of the single core and blocking LED writes; no latency figures — [Elektor Fireflies](https://www.elektormagazine.com/labs/fireflies)
- ESP-NOW latency reports: **[anecdote]** 100-byte structs every 30 ms showed 50 ms average and spikes to ~200 ms — [esp-now issue 115](https://github.com/espressif/esp-now/issues/115); **[anecdote]** 13-device ESP32-C3 test: Espressif staff posted RTT/loss results (image only); users noted RTT rises with distance and that raising the data rate from the 1 Mbps default lowers RTT — [esp32.com p=118112](https://esp32.com/viewtopic.php?p=118112); hobby "hello world" RTT test found latency slightly higher for farther devices and error rate the bigger concern — [Hackaday.io 164132](https://hackaday.io/project/164132-hello-world-for-esp-now)

### Inferences
- Nobody in the hobby space has published a BLE-advertising-based colour-swarm with measured latency; the closest working systems use ESP-NOW because it is pairing-free broadcast. Since this demo must keep the Wi-Fi station connected, ESP-NOW is actually viable on the same radio (it shares the Wi-Fi channel) and is the better-documented low-latency path, but the question assigned here is BLE; the same *patterns* (periodic broadcast, quantised time/state, no acks) transfer directly.

## Key Question 6 — Design patterns for shared live state over lossy broadcast

### Takeaway
Every working lossy-broadcast design surveyed (game netcode, ESP-NOW badges, PixMob, Bluetooth Mesh itself) sends *state* repeatedly rather than *events*, carries a per-sender sequence number so the newest state wins, has receivers expire a sender after a timeout instead of waiting for a "release" message, and renders statelessly from the received state; Bluetooth Mesh adds a formal replay-protection list (last SEQ per source) and a heartbeat/offline timeout.

### Cited Findings
- Gaffer On Games "State Synchronization": each packet carries a sequence number so the receiver can detect "out of order, lost or duplicate packets"; inputs are sent redundantly ("we send multiple redundant inputs"); if the next input is missing the simulation keeps running on the last input rather than waiting; lost state is covered because "state updates for those objects are being continually included in future packets"; the base design uses no acks (acks appear only for delta compression and for "keep resending recently rested objects until the receiver confirms"); a jitter buffer of "4–5 frames @ 60 Hz" smooths packets that "clump up across frames"; "snap the physics state hard" rather than smoothing; a priority accumulator spreads updates across objects under a bandwidth cap — [Gaffer On Games](https://gafferongames.com/post/state_synchronization/)
- **[secondary]** Common netcode rule: continuous state (positions etc.) goes as unreliable datagrams ~20–30 times per second; reliable channel reserved for one-off critical events ("UDP for state, reliability for events") — [systemdr substack](https://systemdr.substack.com/p/udp-vs-tcp-in-multiplayer-gaming); event-based sync requires reliable delivery and buffering, state-based lets the latest snapshot supersede lost ones — [jMonkeyEngine forum](https://hub.jmonkeyengine.org/t/p2p-physics/3802)
- ESP-NOW badge project: broadcast shared clock every ~5 ms; adopt the highest received value (monotonic); render quantised to 50 ms; stateless effect = f(shared time); no acks; late joiners catch up from the next broadcast — [Hackaday.io 205611](https://hackaday.io/project/205611-self-synchronising-led-badges-using-esp-now)
- Bluetooth Mesh replay protection: every node keeps a running 24-bit sequence number per outbound message; each message is unique by (SEQ, source address); receivers store the last SEQ seen per source and reject older ones; default replay-protection list size 32 sources (Nordic and Silicon Labs) — [DevZone 24193](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/f/nordic-q-a/24193/how-does-replay-cache-prevent-replay-attacks-in-ble-mesh/95282); [SiLabs networking principles](https://docs.silabs.com/btmesh/9.0.2/btmesh-network-optimization/03-bluetooth-mesh-networking-principles); Zephyr notes the RPL must be persisted and offers `CONFIG_BT_MESH_RPL_STORE_TIMEOUT` to limit flash wear — [Zephyr mesh core docs](https://codex.ac6.fr/Zephyr/xref/Zephyr-Core-2.7.6/doc/reference/bluetooth/mesh/core.rst)
- Bluetooth Mesh redundancy = Network Transmit (count + 10–320 ms interval), Relay Retransmit, and model Publish Period/Retransmit; Silicon Labs warns "If the publish rate is too high, it can drastically increase the traffic on the network" — [SiLabs networking principles](https://docs.silabs.com/btmesh/9.0.2/btmesh-network-optimization/03-bluetooth-mesh-networking-principles); sent messages follow the Network Transmit state, published messages follow the Model Publication state (period and count) — [ESP-BLE-MESH FAQ 2.7](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/v5.5.1/esp32s3/api-guides/esp-ble-mesh/ble-mesh-faq.html)
- Mesh liveness: a node is considered offline when the Provisioner "failed to receive heartbeat messages in a certain period" (FAQ 1.19) — [ESP-BLE-MESH FAQ](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/v5.5.1/esp32s3/api-guides/esp-ble-mesh/ble-mesh-faq.html)
- Nordic 99-node data: NTC = 2 (3 transmissions at 20 ms) took unacknowledged delivery from 98–99 % to 100 % on the weakest nodes — [Nordic blog](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/nordic/nordic-blog/b/blog/posts/large-scale-bluetooth-mesh-testing)
- Espressif staff on lossy scanning under Wi-Fi coexistence: repeat advertisements (suggested ~35 ms spacing) so at least one copy lands while the radio is listening — [esp32.com p=126397](https://esp32.com/viewtopic.php?p=126397)
- PixMob: controller keeps repeating; band behaviour (blink rate, wake) is a function of repetition; per-band randomness is computed locally from a seed in the packet tail — [GitHub pixmob-ir-reverse-engineering](https://github.com/danielweidman/pixmob-ir-reverse-engineering)
- Kindt: with scan window = scan interval, worst-case discovery of one advertiser is one advertising interval — [ar5iv 1509.04366](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1509.04366); advDelay adds 0–10 ms per event — [DevZone 85629](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/f/nordic-q-a/85629/nature-of-pseudo-random-delay-advdelay-0-10-ms-added-to-the-advertising-interval)

### Inferences (recommended pattern for the demo, derived from the above)
- **Payload = state, not event**: `{sender_id, seq (8–16 bit), holding (1 bit), R, G, B}` fits in a legacy 31-byte advertisement with room to spare and stays ≤ 11 B if sent as a mesh access message (avoids segmentation, Q1).
- **Cadence**: while holding, re-broadcast every 20–50 ms (badges used ~5 ms over ESP-NOW; 20 ms is the mesh advInterval default; Espressif suggests ~35 ms spacing under coexistence). On release, send `holding = 0` at least 3–5 times over ≥ 100–150 ms (longer than one Wi-Fi coexistence period, > 100 ms) and then keep a slow idle beacon (e.g., 500 ms–1 s) so late joiners and recovered receivers learn the current colour.
- **Receiver**: last-writer-wins per sender via `seq` (wrap-aware compare, as mesh RPL does); consider a sender released when either `holding = 0` is received or no packet has arrived for an expiry window. With a 50 ms cadence and ~1–4 % per-packet loss in a clean room (Q2), three consecutive misses is ~10⁻⁴–10⁻⁵ probability, so an expiry of 3 missed intervals (150 ms) is safe in clean air; under Wi-Fi coexistence loss streaks reach 100 % for a period, so the expiry should be ≥ 1 coexistence period (> 100 ms) plus margin — 200–300 ms is a reasonable starting point, with the explicit release flag making the common case fast **[estimate]**.
- **Render statelessly** from the set of currently-held (colour) entries each frame, as badges and PixMob do; never queue events. Quantise or low-pass the mix on the display (badges found 50 ms steps hid ESP-NOW jitter; a 100–200 ms fade on add/remove also hides the Q1 tail outliers).
- **Jitter**: no jitter buffer is needed for an on/off colour state (Gaffer's jitter buffer is for continuous motion); a short fade-in/fade-out is the equivalent.

### Gaps
- No published "how many missed beacons before declaring a peer gone" rule for BLE advertising swarms; the expiry numbers above are derived, not measured.
- Bluetooth Mesh's own model-level "transition time"/"delay" fields (Generic OnOff/Level) were not researched here.

## Key Question 7 — Mixing several senders' colours on one display

### Takeaway
Physical additive mixing is a sum of light; on a display with a fixed maximum, a plain per-channel sum must be clamped (what FastLED's saturating `qadd8`/`CRGB +=` does), averaging keeps range but halves brightness per source and desaturates, and normalising the sum so the largest channel hits full scale keeps hue and saturation at constant brightness. Mixing in gamma-encoded sRGB makes midpoints dark; mix in linear light (or at least avoid HSV hue averaging, which needs a circular mean).

### Cited Findings
- Wikipedia: additive mixing yields brightness equal to the *sum* of the components; "average mixing" (spatial/temporal dithering faster than the eye resolves) yields brightness equal to the *average* — [Wikipedia Color mixing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_mixing)
- Monitors have a maximum intensity, so the sum of two (255,0,0) reds cannot be displayed; averaging is offered as a reasonable compromise — [Straight Dope forum](https://boards.straightdope.com/t/ms-windows-colors-and-arithmetic/133713)
- A graphics patent normalises each source's RGB to 0.0–1.0 before combining, stating it gives a more realistic effect than plain additive summation, which "breaks down when more than a few light sources are present" — [US 6018350](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6018350)
- FastLED: `qadd8` is a saturating 8-bit add ("if the sum exceeds 255, it's automatically clamped to 255"; qadd8(200,200) = 255); `CRGB +=` adds R, G and B channels with the saturating method; the function was added precisely for "adding the three led channel colors"; `scale8(val, 100)` rescales 0–255 to 0–100; `scale8_video` keeps non-zero inputs non-zero — [FastLED wiki](https://github.com/FastLED/FastLED/wiki/High-performance-math); `nblend` is a fractional blend toward another colour, not an add — [FastLED docs](https://fastled.io/docs/group___color_blends.html)
- Linear-light vs sRGB: "a checkerboard between red and blue will look brighter than the average of their sRGB values" because sRGB values are not light intensities — [silverhammermba gamma post](https://silverhammermba.github.io/blog/2017/12/01/gamma); OBS: blending with gamma 1.0 (linear) "removes the dark artifacts you would normally see where colors would meet" — [OBS wiki](https://obsproject.com/wiki/Linear-Color-Blending); red→green gradient has a dark midpoint in gamma space but yellow in linear — [GIMP forum](https://www.gimp-forum.net/Thread-All-my-color-blendings-are-tending-towards-brighter-values-in-all-images); Mozilla attributes dark gradient mid-tones to interpolation in gamma-encoded sRGB — [Bugzilla 1248178](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1248178); counter-view: gamma-space averaging can look closer to a *perceptual* midpoint, and Photoshop offers both — [pixls.us](https://discuss.pixls.us/t/exposure-fusion-and-intel-neo-drivers/13347/22), [W3C colorweb list, Lars Borg](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-colorweb/2021Jan/0051.html)
- HSV averaging: naive hue mean of 40° and 300° lands on the wrong side of the wheel; fix is the circular mean (average sin/cos, atan2) — [iter.ca hue-avg](https://iter.ca/post/hue-avg/); example: red (0°) + blue (240°) circular mean = 300° (magenta), not 120° (green); grey has undefined hue and must be excluded — [culors test](https://docs.rs/crate/culors/1.6.0/source/tests/ported_average.rs)

### Inferences
- For "several holders mix on my screen", the behaviour that matches the physical intuition (two torches on one wall) is **sum in linear light, then handle overflow**. Two options: (a) clamp (FastLED `+=`): red + green → yellow at full brightness, but red + red → red (no change), and three saturated colours → near-white, which reads as "more people = brighter/whiter" and loses who-is-holding; (b) **sum then divide by the largest channel so max = 1.0** (patent-style normalisation): keeps hue and saturation of the mix, constant full brightness, red + green → full yellow, red + blue → full magenta, red + green + blue → white. Option (b) is the better default for a display where brightness is the headline cue; add a subtle brightness or ring-segment cue for "how many" if needed **[design inference]**.
- Plain averaging (sum ÷ N) halves brightness with two holders and quarters it with four; it "looks dim" because displays cannot exceed full scale and the average of two saturated primaries in sRGB is a muddy mid-tone (the gamma problem above). Doing the average in linear light and re-encoding fixes the dark midpoint but not the dimming.
- Avoid averaging hue in HSV unless using the circular mean, and even then red + cyan (opposite hues) has an undefined mean — the linear-RGB sum handles that case gracefully (it gives white/grey).
- On the device, the cheap pipeline is: for each held colour, sRGB → linear (LUT, 256 entries), accumulate in 16-bit, find max channel, scale so max = 1.0 (or clamp), linear → sRGB (LUT), optionally low-pass with FastLED-style `nblend` at ~100–200 ms to hide packet jitter — all integer math; FastLED shows the saturating-add variant is "smaller, easier to read and write, and faster" in assembly.

### Gaps
- No source was found that states how a specific commercial multi-source light product (Nanoleaf scenes, PixMob overlapping groups) computes its mix; PixMob's answer to overlap is "the pixel will just not react" (IR collision), not a mix.
- The "sum then normalise by max channel" rule is supported only by the graphics patent and general colour theory, not by a user study on round displays; treat it as a design default to validate by mock-up.
